Call to Action
1. Choose the audience, topic, and hook
Audiences + audience knowledge, goals, concerns, and likely misconceptions:
University students 
Knowledge: Likely use AI tools for school or for project, may have heard about ai safety or learned about risks through campus events or recently through social media 
Goals: A meaningful career, internships, clear next steps, community
Concerns: Not being qualified yet, picking the wrong path, AI impacting their ability to find a job
Misconceptions: That you need to be very qualified to try to start a career in AI safety, that you can’t have an impact until you graduate, that there are not many stable career paths in ai safety
Notes: Depending on the field of study some of the contents of this section might differ and they will need different information, but the later pathway sections (technical, policy, ops, etc.) apply to students in those fields too. This section focuses only on what's unique to being a student.
Technical people 
Knowledge: Understand to certain extent how models work
Goals: Interested in technical problems, good pay, credibility, and work that matters
Concerns: A pay cut, whether working in technical safety speeds up capabilities
Misconceptions: That the only option is working at a frontier lab, that they’ll need lots of retraining, 
Notes: Overlaps with technical students so they can share pathways. Important to mention pathways that are not labs
Policy and governance people 
Knowledge: Know how legislation, regulation, and institutions work but may have little technical understanding of AI
Goals: Influence real decisions, advance their careers, work on big issues
Concerns: Lacking technical credibility, AI safety and regulation being a fad, and not knowing what organizations exist and which are serious
Misconceptions: That AI policy is working on privacy, bias, or copyright. That you need to code. That it’s too early to matter, that governance work only happens in DC
Notes: technical literacy helps and can be built quickly though existing courses, fellowships, and reading groups
Non technical or policy mid-career professionals
Knowledge: They have deep expertise in their own field and follow AI news casually. They're worried but not connected to the community.
Goals: Use the skills they've built over years, find meaningful work, and avoid starting from scratch.
Concerns: Losing seniority or pay, feeling too old, and whether the field will take them seriously.
Misconceptions: That AI safety only needs researchers. That the field only hires young people. That they'd have to go back to school. That their expertise doesn't transfer, when infosec, comms, and management are frequently cited as needs.
Notes:
Operations
Knowledge: They know how to run things like hiring, finance, events, and project management, but probably don't see ops as "AI safety work."
Goals: Impact through enabling others, stability, and growth into leadership roles.
Concerns: Being seen as support staff rather than core, whether small organizations are stable, and unclear career growth.
Misconceptions: That ops is less impactful than research in AI safety when in reality, many organizations say ops and management are a bottleneck. Another misconception is that you need to understand the technical details deeply to do the job well.
Notes: 
Concerned general public
Knowledge: Their understanding comes from headlines, movies, and social media. They feel either anxious or dismissive.
Goals: Feel less helpless, understand what's actually going on, and do something.
Concerns: That nothing they do matters, being overwhelmed by doom, and not knowing whom to trust.
Misconceptions: That it's either all fine or all hopeless. That only tech people can help. That individual actions like calling representatives, voting, or donating don't matter. That AI risk just means job loss or deepfakes.
Notes:
Founders and entrepreneurs 
Knowledge: They know how to build organizations and products from scratch, but may see AI safety as purely academic or nonprofit research.
Goals: Build something new, have impact, find success, and own a problem.
Concerns: Whether there's a viable market or funding model, and whether nonprofit work fits their skills.
Misconceptions: That AI safety has no room for startups or new orgs. That all the important organizations already exist. That founding only means for-profit companies.
Notes: AI Safety Undervalues Founders. Possible examples include new evals orgs, field-building groups, and safety tooling. 
Journalists, writers, and content creators 
Knowledge: They know how to explain things to audiences and may already cover tech, but often lack depth on AI risk.
Goals: Tell important stories, build an audience, and gain credibility on a growing beat.
Concerns: Being seen as alarmist, lacking technical expertise, and whether there's a sustainable career covering this.
Misconceptions: That covering AI means product launches and hype. That you need a technical background to report on AI risk. That AI safety has nothing to do with communications.
Notes: Tarbell is a direct example. 
Academics outside CS(econ, bio…)
Knowledge: They have deep expertise in their discipline and research skills, but may not see how it connects to AI.
Goals: Do research that matters, get funding and publications, and stay in their field.
Concerns: Leaving their discipline, whether AI work counts in their field's career track, and lacking ML knowledge.
Misconceptions: That they'd have to switch to CS or policy work. That their field has nothing to contribute. Examples of relevant work include economics of AI, biosecurity, nuclear-style arms control analogies, AI law, and philosophy of alignment.
Notes: Overlaps with non-CS students, so both can share the "apply your field" pathway.
Topic / central question:
If you’re worried about AI, what can you do, from simple steps to building a career doing work that matters?
Hook:
“So you are scared about AI! What can you do, from simple steps to building a career doing work that matters?"
Unsolicited hook idea from Calvin: Something like… “James is an influencer. Tara is an operations manager. Morgan translates Chinese legal documents. They’re all among the most impactful people in the world working on preventing catastrophic AI risks. Here’s how…” Lean into the ‘lots of weird and unexpected AI safety jobs’ hook. Maybe choose some more exciting sounding jobs though lol

Day-to-day levers
I am personally pretty unfamiliar with what AI safety political boots-on-ground advocacy actually looks like, so instead of a link dump I’ve structured this section as a list of concrete things one can do, with the source linked.
Constituent legislative outreach. Schedule in-district or virtual lawmaker meetings with specific statutory asks (compute thresholds, liability, whistleblower protections) using advocacy-group letter/meeting builders. Source: PauseAI UK constituent writeup
Public commenting on regulatory rulemakings. File individualized comments during federal/state notice-and-comment windows (Commerce, NIST, FTC, AG offices) tying safety claims to administrative-law reasoning. Source: catastrophe prevention/governance spend
Local grassroots mobilization & tabling. Run campus/community tables, peaceful demonstrations, and education drops; microgrants can cover print/banners. Source: One Year of PauseAI UK
Sub-national & municipal AI policy engagement. Show up to city council, school board, or local tech task forces to push bounded procurement, transparency, and cautious automation in critical services. Source: How to get into AI policy (part 4)
Public discourse reframing & content synthesis. Write op-eds/newsletters linking mundane harms (deepfakes, scams, labor shocks) to structural control risks and summarize dense evals for journalists. Source: Stop chasing views
Study circles & local group facilitation. Co-run BlueDot/AGISF-style cohorts to onboard lawyers, engineers, and mid-career professionals into safety literacy. Source: 57 AI policy upskilling resources
Caveats and other interesting points 
Staff often read one engaged constituent as standing in for hundreds of silent voters, so sparse but prepared local meetings punch above their headcount (same post).
Dont wait for an AI "warning shot" to do the persuasion; without prior public framing, crises get misattributed or normalized instead of triggering safety rules (warning shots essay).
Sloppy or hyper-partisan grassroots tone can wedge AI safety and net-harm despite good intentions (scouts vs soldiers).
Notice-and-comment dockets reward individualized administrative-law arguments; mass copied form letters get grouped and discarded (EA Forum on catastrophe prevention/governance spend).

General points
General points
1. AI safety needs many kinds of people, not just researchers
Roles include research, engineering, policy, law, security, operations, management, communications, journalism, grantmaking, fieldbuilding, economics, biosecurity, design, founding organizations, and more.
AI safety organizations also hire people, run events, raise money, and share their ideas, and none of that requires linear algebra (80,000 Hours).
An analysis of 3,654 job postings found that leadership roles most need management plus ops, strategy, or policy skills, and that infosec is a key skill at AI labs (EA Forum).
Caveat: research is still the most common need, and pipelines into non-research roles are less developed.
2. Lots of people care, and the field is growing fast
The field has grown about 3000% in the last eight years (80,000 Hours).
BlueDot has trained over 10,000 professionals since 2022 (BlueDot).
Caveat: the field is still small relative to the problem, which also means there's lots of room to grow.
3. It's a well-funded field
Some philanthropists hope to deploy tens of billions of dollars on these risks in the coming years (80,000 Hours).
Funders need people to spend it. Millions are available for people who can launch AI safety nonprofits, and the hard part is finding founders (80,000 Hours podcast).
This supports well-paid roles. Horizon fellows earn $78K to $190K+ (Horizon).
Caveat: pay varies a lot by role, nonprofits usually pay less than labs, and much of the funding comes from a few large funders.
4. There are many ways to learn, often free
BlueDot's courses are free. The 2-hour intro has no application, and longer courses require one.
BlueDot courses were the most-mentioned upskilling step in a survey of 38 AI safety leaders (80,000 Hours).
Other resources: the free ARENA curriculum, podcasts, reading groups, and SPAR's reading list.
Caveat: courses build knowledge, but they don't get you hired alone. Pair them with projects and networking.
5. You can start fast, and doing the work matters more than credentials
An 80,000 Hours advisor thinks many driven people can pivot within three months (80,000 Hours).
Example: Luca noticed a gap, built a career platform on his own, and was hired by 80,000 Hours (same source).
Caveat: three months is a best case. Senior and specialized roles often take longer.
6. Be persistent
Rejection is normal. Many research fellowships accept under 3% of applicants (Fieldbuilders).
Many people who felt they had nothing to contribute landed AI safety jobs a few months later (80,000 Hours).
Keep building context, joining groups, and working on projects. Public work gets you noticed.
Caveat: demand is uneven. Entry-level research is crowded, while ops, management, security, and founding have bigger gaps.
